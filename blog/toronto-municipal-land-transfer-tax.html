Toronto's Municipal Land Transfer Tax: What First-Time Buyers Actually Pay

Every home sale in Ontario comes with a provincial land transfer tax. If the property is in Toronto, there's a second one on top of it - the Municipal Land Transfer Tax (MLTT) - and Toronto is the only municipality in the province that charges it. A buyer closing on a condo in Mississauga or a house in Hamilton pays one land transfer tax. A buyer closing a few kilometres away in Toronto pays two, calculated separately, on the same purchase price.

Why Toronto and nowhere else

The city gained the authority to levy its own land transfer tax under the City of Toronto Act, and has applied it to resale and new-construction purchases since 2008. Every other Ontario municipality relies on the province's tax alone. It's a detail that surprises a lot of out-of-town buyers, and it's one of the first things a real estate lawyer will flag when they see a Toronto address on the agreement of purchase and sale - because it changes the closing-cost math buyers usually plan around.

What the first-time buyer rebate actually covers

Both levels of tax offer a rebate for qualifying first-time buyers, and both rebates apply separately - one against the provincial tax, one against the municipal tax. Neither rebate typically covers the full tax bill on an average-priced Toronto home once you're above the rebate threshold; it reduces the bill rather than eliminating it. The eligibility rules (Canadian citizenship or permanent residency, never having owned a home anywhere, and in some cases a spouse's ownership history counting against you) and the exact rebate ceilings are adjusted periodically, so treat any number you see online as a starting point and have your lawyer confirm the current figures before you budget around them.

Where this actually gets missed

The MLTT applies based on the property's location, not the buyer's residency or where the deal was negotiated. Buyers relocating to Toronto from elsewhere in Ontario sometimes budget using land transfer tax calculators that don't separate the two levies, and the shortfall shows up as an unpleasant surprise on closing day rather than at the offer stage, when there's still room to negotiate. A lawyer reviewing the agreement before you sign - not after - is the point at which this gets caught.
