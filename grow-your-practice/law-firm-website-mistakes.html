7 Website Mistakes That Cost Law Firms New Clients

Website

A law firm website doesn't need to be flashy to work well, but a handful of common, fixable mistakes quietly cost firms inquiries every day - often without anyone noticing, because a visitor who leaves without calling doesn't show up anywhere in the analytics as a problem.

1. No visible phone number above the fold

If a visitor has to scroll or hunt for a phone number, some meaningful share of them simply leave instead. The number should be visible in the header on every page, on both desktop and mobile, ideally as a tappable link on mobile so it dials directly.

2. One page trying to cover every practice area

A single "Practice Areas" page with a paragraph per service tells Google (and a visitor) far less than a dedicated page per practice area actually would, and it makes the firm look less specialized than firms with focused, individual pages for each service they offer.

3. No clear mention of the cities or regions actually served

A visitor unsure whether a firm actually serves their city often just moves to the next search result rather than calling to ask. Explicitly naming the cities and regions served - ideally on relevant practice-area pages, not just a generic "about" page - removes that uncertainty.

4. Stock photography with zero specifics

Generic stock photos of gavels and handshakes don't build any more trust than a photo-free page, and real photos of the actual office and (with consent) the actual lawyers tend to perform noticeably better, because they give a prospective client something concrete to connect with before they've spoken to anyone.

5. Slow load times on mobile

A majority of legal searches now happen on a phone, often from someone in a stressful situation who won't wait long for a page to load. Large unoptimized images and heavy, unnecessary scripts are the most common cause of a slow mobile site, and both are usually fixable without a full rebuild.

6. No clear next step

A page that explains services well but doesn't tell the visitor what to actually do next - call, book a consultation, fill out a form - leaves the decision entirely up to them at the exact moment they need the least friction possible. A clear, repeated call to action (not just at the very bottom of a long page) makes the next step obvious.

7. Ignoring what happens after the form is submitted

A contact form that sends to an inbox nobody checks promptly undoes all the work of getting someone to fill it out in the first place. Confirming that form submissions actually reach someone quickly - and that someone follows up same-day - is worth checking directly rather than assuming it's working.
